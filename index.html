Assignment 2

What drives a country’s happiness up or down?

Using World Happiness Report data for 2012–2025, this piece answers the question on three levels. Long-term trend: which regions are catching up and which are falling behind (Figure 1). Structure: how much of the difference in happiness income can explain (Figure 2). Shocks: whether war or economic collapse hits happiness harder (Figure 3).

Figure 1

Central & Eastern Europe closed nearly 70% of its happiness gap with Western Europe, while North America fell below it

Central & Eastern Europe (17 countries) Western Europe North America (US, Canada) Australia & New Zealand Other 7 regions Gap between CEE and Western Europe

Insight 1:Using the WHR’s ten-region classification, all 17 Central & Eastern European (CEE) countries saw their scores rise, by an average of 1.07 points, and the region’s gap with Western Europe narrowed from 1.56 to 0.49 points, closing by nearly 70%. Western Europe itself barely moved. North America (the United States and Canada) led Western Europe by 0.44 points in 2012, was overtaken from 2023 onward, and in 2025 scored 0.07 points below it; Canada dropped from 6th to 25th place. Australia and New Zealand are also declining, but still lead Western Europe by 0.11 points.

Figure 2

Money can’t buy all of happiness: Latin America is “poor but happy”, Hong Kong “rich but unhappy”

Latin America (17 countries) Rich East Asia (Japan, South Korea, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore) Other Score predicted by income alone Larger dot = higher health contribution

Insight 2:Compared with what their income levels predict, all 17 Latin American countries score higher, by 0.75 points on average. This shows that people there are happier than people in countries with similar incomes; lower incomes have not dragged their happiness down. The five rich East Asian economies (Japan, South Korea, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore) all score below or close to the prediction, by 0.57 points on average, showing that their high incomes have not brought a matching level of happiness. Taiwan sits almost exactly on the prediction line, meaning its happiness roughly matches its income. Hong Kong scores 1.18 points below its prediction, the largest shortfall among all rich economies, showing that the gap between income and happiness is most pronounced there.

Figure 3

War does not mean a collapse in happiness; economic collapse hits happiness harder

Countries at war (9) Economic collapse (3) War + economic collapse (Afghanistan) Other Larger marker = bigger rebound since the low of its largest drop

Insight 3:The nine countries that experienced war (Ukraine, Russia, Israel, Ethiopia, Myanmar, Burkina Faso, Somalia, Nigeria, Yemen) did see their happiness scores fall, but none dropped by more than 1.1 points at any point since 2012. War lowers happiness, but in most cases it did not make it collapse; Israel fell by only 0.29 points and still ranked 8th in 2025. In the countries with the deepest falls, the decline coincided with economic collapse (external context). Lebanon held steady at around 5.2 until 2018; after its financial crisis began in 2019, its score fell to 2.39 by 2022, and during the 2023–24 conflict it actually recovered to 3.72. Its collapse came with the economic crisis, not with the war. Venezuela fell from 7.04 in 2012 to 4.71 in 2018, during the hyperinflation that followed the oil-price crash, showing that economic collapse alone, without war, can push happiness down sharply. Lebanon and Venezuela both appear as large squares because they have since rebounded from their lowest points by 1.33 and 1.05 points: the deepest falls came with economic collapse, but happiness recovered noticeably once the crisis eased. Afghanistan is the exception. It had already fallen from 4.04 to 2.52 during the war before 2021, then dropped to 1.36 after the 2021 change of government and economic collapse, so the effects of war and economic collapse cannot be separated there. Because each score is a three-year survey average, declines show up slightly later than the events themselves; this comparison rests on a few cases and shows correlation, not causation.

Summary:A common assumption is that richer countries and regions are happier, and that war inevitably sends people’s happiness plunging. This analysis finds otherwise. Happiness in wealthy North America has broadly declined, while Central & Eastern Europe keeps rising. At similar income levels, Latin America is happier and Hong Kong less happy, showing that income does not fully determine happiness and that other factors play a part. Finally, war lowers happiness but mostly does not make it collapse; the real cliff-edge falls tend to come with economic collapse, when the security of everyday life is lost. These are correlations observed in the data, not causal conclusions.

Data notes

  • Source: World Happiness Report 2026, Figure 2.1 data (2011–2025). Life evaluation is scored 0–10.
  • Each year’s score is the average of that year’s survey and the two before it (e.g. 2025 = average of 2023–2025, published in WHR 2026). No report was published in 2014, so there is no 2013 data point.
  • Figure 1: the 129 countries with data in every edition, 2012–2025. Regions follow the WHR’s ten-region classification; North America and Australia & New Zealand are each the average of two countries.
  • Figure 2: 2025 cross-section. The dashed line is an income-prediction line calculated by the author: an ordinary least-squares regression (n = 146) of each country’s life evaluation on the dataset’s “Explained by: Log GDP per capita” column, giving predicted score = 1.55 + 2.65 × GDP contribution. 1.55 is the intercept and 2.65 the slope (each 1-unit increase in GDP contribution raises the predicted score by 2.65 points); R² = 0.64, meaning the line explains about 64% of the variation in scores between countries. The WHR builds this column from log GDP per capita, so the horizontal axis is equivalent to log income. Each country’s “above / below income prediction” = actual score − predicted score. Venezuela is excluded because its GDP is recorded as 0 from 2021, most likely missing data. “Latin America” is defined by the author as the 17 Latin American countries in the WHR’s “Latin America & Caribbean” region, excluding Jamaica, the Dominican Republic and Trinidad and Tobago (including them, 19 of 20 score above the prediction). “Rich East Asia” is also a grouping defined by the author.
  • Figure 3: the horizontal axis is the largest drop since 2012 from a high point to a later low point. The war and economic-collapse groups are classified by the author from external sources; war means at least 1,000 battle deaths in a year (UCDP definition). Marker size is the 2025 score minus the low point reached in that same largest drop. Syria, Sudan and Haiti have no recent data and are not included. For some countries at war the largest drop does not fall in the war years: Russia’s (2016→2021) came before 2022, and Burkina Faso’s (2012→2014) came before its conflict escalated.
  • In Figure 2, “above the prediction” means happier than income alone would predict. It does not mean a “better culture” and may reflect factors such as survey response styles. Small countries such as Belize have wide confidence intervals, so read the overall pattern rather than individual countries.
  • In Figure 3, countries at war were not unaffected: their median largest drop is 0.70, higher than 0.39 for other countries.
  • All relationships shown are correlations, not causation.
